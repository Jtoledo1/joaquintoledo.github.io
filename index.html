About

I am a PhD student in Economics at Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile. My research lies in urban economics, with a particular focus on land-use regulation, housing markets, and urban development. Methodologically, I rely heavily on microeconometric techniques and spatial data to study how regulatory constraints shape prices, supply, and city structure.

Beyond academia, I have worked as a consultant on public policy, collaborating with public sector institutions on issues related to urban regulation, housing, and local development. I also have teaching experience in quantitative methods and economics.

I am originally from Uruguay. Outside of research, I play basketball at an amateur level, enjoy solving puzzles, and am a long-time supporter of Peñarol.

Working Papers

Property Taxes as Market Signals: Evidence from Housing Markets with Information Frictions
Hugo E. Silva, Joaquín Toledo Tesoro
SSRN

This paper estimates the causal effect of property tax liability on housing prices using georeferenced transaction and cadastral data from Santiago, Chile. Using a sharp regression discontinuity design (RDD) at the exemption threshold, we document a novel “tax premium”: becoming liable for property tax raises average housing prices by approximately 4 percent. We hypothesize that tax liability acts as a third-party certification that reduces information asymmetry, thereby overcoming the negative capitalization of tax costs. We evaluate this mechanism using three complementary tests. First, we systematically rule out alternative structural channels such as increased local public spending and social sorting. Second, we find no evidence that being liable to pay property taxes serves as a status signal or a coordination device for social sorting. Third, we show that the positive effect is driven by the extensive margin (being liable to taxes). By contrast, when we estimate the impact at the intensive margin using tax-rate changes at a higher threshold, we find the standard negative capitalization effect. Finally, by categorizing transactions by the magnitude of informational opacity derived from a hedonic price model, we find that the tax premium is negligible in transparent segments and is large and significant for properties with high informational friction. These findings suggest that in developing markets with informational gaps, fiscal tools can inadvertently serve as market signals, reversing standard incidence predictions.


Property Taxes and Rental Prices: Evidence from a Large-Scale Cadastral Reassessment
Matias Brum, Joaquín Toledo Tesoro
SSRN

This paper examines the causal effect of property tax increases on rental prices in Uruguay, exploiting a quasi-natural experiment from a large-scale cadastral reassessment in Canelones in 2011. Using a Synthetic Difference-in-Differences (SDID) estimator, we find the reassessment raised rental prices by 9.3% relative to a synthetic counterfactual, with effects becoming statistically significant three to four years post-implementation. The 21% average increase in taxable property values produced a pass-through rate of roughly 44%, consistent with incomplete adjustment in markets with inelastic housing supply. Leveraging housing demand elasticities from the existing empirical literature, our observed pass-through implies supply elasticities of 0.29–0.32, indicating relatively inelastic supply in Canelones' mixed urban-suburban-rural context. We also document a 12.4% rise in imputed rental values (valor locativo) for homeowners, reflecting broader market contagion. These findings highlight institutional constraints—particularly rental contract regulations that delay adjustment—shaping the timing and magnitude of tax pass-through, contributing to the literature on tax incidence in regulated housing markets and informing local fiscal policy design.


When Do Land-Use Regulations Matter? Stringency, Compliance, and Urban Development in Santiago
Joaquín Toledo Tesoro, Diego Gil, Hugo Silva
PDF (coming soon)

Does land-use regulation actually bind? And if so, through which instruments and under which institutional conditions? We study these questions in Greater Santiago. Using a rich regulatory panel covering 1990–2019, combined with building permits (2010–2020) and land transactions (2005–2020), we implement a shadow-price framework to measure the economic stringency of three key regulatory instruments: floor-area ratio (FAR), height limits, and occupancy requirements. We find that FAR is consistently binding across the city, with estimated price elasticities of 0.65–0.80. Height restrictions, by contrast, generate significant price effects only where local enforcement is strong; in low-compliance municipalities, height caps are perceived as a source of regulatory uncertainty rather than a credible constraint, and are capitalized as a price discount. Occupancy requirements are effectively non-binding everywhere. These results show that regulatory impact depends jointly on the instrument used and whether it is actually enforced—a finding with direct implications for housing policy in cities where formal rules and effective enforcement diverge.
